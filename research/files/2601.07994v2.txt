5.3 Evaluation Metrics
We evaluate context management methods using
three metrics: Answer Quality, Response Latency,
and Retrieval Accuracy.
Answer Quality We adopt GPT4Score, follow-
ing prior work (Lu et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025;
Yuan et al., 2025), in which GPT-4 serves as a
judge and assigns a score from 1 to 100 based
on how well the model response answers the user
question. This aligns our evaluation with common
practice and provides consistent, scalable assess-
ments. The evaluation prompt (Figure 3) provides
GPT-4 with the conversation history, user ques-
tion, model response, and gold answer as input.

Response Latency We measure online first-
token latency—the time from receiving the user
utterance to generating the first token of the model
response—to quantify the additional latency intro-
duced by each memory management method. To
observe when the first token is generated, we call
all LLMs in streaming mode.
Retrieval Accuracy We use Hit@k, Recall@k,
and Precision@k to evaluate how accurately each
method retrieves the context relevant to the cur-
rent turn. As only LoCoMo benchmark provides
ground-truth annotations of relevant context for
each test question, we use LoCoMo for this evalu-
ation. Each metric is defined as follows:
• Hit@k indicates whether any gold context
appears in the top-k results.
• Recall@k is the proportion of gold contexts
covered by the top-k results.
• Precision@k is the proportion of the top-k
results that include gold context.
5.4 Experimental Details
For the implementation of DYCP, we set the gain
threshold τ to 0.6 and the stopping threshold θ
to 1.0 as hyperparameters of the KadaneDial.
These values were chosen based on preliminary
qualitative inspection of retrieved context spans
and were found to produce stable pruning behav-
ior. Because the relevance scores are z-score nor-
malized before span selection, the thresholds op-
erate on a standardized scale. As a result, DYCP
is not overly sensitive to the specific numerical
choices of τ and θ, and we therefore use the same
configuration across all datasets.
To ensure a fair comparison in retrieval-based
baselines, we match the total number of retrieved
turns across methods wherever applicable. For
segment-level approaches such as SeCom, we
align their retrieval volume with that of DYCP by
matching the product of TpS and RS reported in
Table 8. Concretely, this corresponds to retriev-
ing the top 5 turns on LoCoMo, 2 on SCM4LLMs,
and 3 on MT-Bench+. MemoChat performs au-
tomatic segment retrieval and therefore does not
require specifying a fixed k. For turn-level re-
trievers such as SCM4LLMs and CondMem, we
follow the original papers and use k=5 and k=3,
respectively, for all benchmarks. In all methods,
retrieved items are reordered chronologically (i.e.,
according to their original dialogue order) before
being provided to the conversational agent.
All experiments with API-based LLMs were
conducted using a single NVIDIA RTX A6000
GPU to run retriever, while experiments involv-
ing open-source LLMs were run locally on four
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.
5.5 Result
5.5.1 Answer Quality & Response Latency
We evaluate our method on three long-form
benchmarks using five conversational agents, in-
cluding two standard models (GPT-4o, Claude
3.7), a lightweight variant (GPT-4o mini), and two
recently released models (GPT-4.1, Claude 4.0).
As shown in Table 4, DYCP consistently
achieves the highest answer quality across bench-
marks and conversational agents, while maintain-
ing low response latency. Overall, performance
generally follows the order: DYCP > Full Con-
text > SeCom > SCM4LLMs > MemoChat, with
both DYCP and SeCom distinguished by their low
latency. Note that, as discussed in Section 5.2.1,
prior studies used short-context LLMs (e.g., 4k-
token models), where the Full Context baseline
might have required truncation. In contrast, our
setting uses long-context LLMs that can ingest the
entire dialogue history, which naturally shifts the
relative performance of the baselines.
The latency advantages of DYCP directly fol-
low from the computational cost analysis in Sec-
tion 5.2.2. Unlike other methods that introduce
additional LLM calls during memory construc-
tion, control, or retrieval refinement, DYCP per-
forms no extra LLM calls—neither offline nor
at query time. This eliminates the overhead of
repeatedly reprocessing the dialogue and avoids
the variance introduced by multi-stage pipelines.
Consequently, the low and stable latency observed
across all agents reflects a structural property of
the method: DYCP both minimizes the number of

5.5.2 Human Evaluation
To validate whether GPT4Score aligns with hu-
man judgments, one of the authors conducted
a separate evaluation on one LoCoMo dialogue
(50 test QA pairs) for responses generated by all
six methods: No Context, Full Context, DYCP,
Secom, MemoChat, and SCM4LLMs. For each
test question, the annotator was shown the user
query, the gold answer, and the model-generated
answer, with the method identity hidden, and as-
signed a correctness label from −1,0,1, where −1
indicates an incorrect answer, 0 a partially correct
answer 8, and 1 a correct answer. As shown in
Figure 5, GPT4Score shows consistent differences
in distributions across the three human labels and
exhibits strong correlations with the human judg-
ments across all methods (r = 0.417–0.842, p <
0.01), confirming that it provides a reliable ap-
proximation of human evaluation.

A key difference among segment-level methods
lies in how segment boundaries are constructed.
SeCom and MemoChat perform segmentation of-
fline, prior to the current turn. This introduces
two limitations: (i) segmentation cannot adapt to
the specific information needs of each query, and
(ii) coarse, pre-defined segments may mix relevant
and irrelevant turns, adding noise during retrieval.
In contrast, DYCP defines segments on demand
based on relevance dynamics, allowing boundaries
to shift depending on the current query.

Here, “finer granularity” does not simply mean re-
trieving more or shorter segments; rather, it re-
flects DYCP’s ability to isolate compact, coherent
spans whose boundaries align with query-relevant
changes in discourse. Such query-adaptive seg-

and improving retrieval precision. This con-
trast is particularly evident on the LoCoMo—the
benchmark with the longest multi-topic conversa-

likely to mismatch the discourse structure. Ac-
cordingly, DYCP outperforms other methods in

5.6 Open-Source LLMs Evaluation
We further examine whether DYCP generalizes to
open-source long-context models, and also eval-
uate CondMem under the same setting, since its
preprocessing step requires extensive LLM calls
(see Section 5.2.2). We conduct experiments on
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (1M context) and Mistral-
Nemo-Instruct (128k context). As shown in Ta-
ble 7, DYCP consistently achieves the best an-
swer quality across all three benchmarks, out-
performing both Full Context and CondMem.
While DYCP maintains low and stable online la-
tency, CondMem shows substantially higher la-
tency with large variance due to its iterative
retrieval-refinement process. Although CondMem
reports high ROUGE-1/2/L scores on Qwen, these
gains are largely driven by keyword overlap: the
method often reproduces short 1–2-gram frag-
ments from the gold answer without providing the
correct factual content. This inflates ROUGE de-
spite low semantic accuracy, as reflected in both
EM and GPT4Score. These results demonstrate
that DYCP remains effective across models, in-
cluding smaller open-source LLMs.
In addition, the results reveal a practical latency
advantage: when running open-source models on
modest local GPUs, passing the full dialogue as
input leads to substantially higher latency com-
pared to API-based LLMs. By reducing the input
context, DYCP mitigates this penalty and yields
a dramatic reduction in end-to-end inference time,
making long-context conversation far more feasi-
ble in local deployment settings.
6 Analysis
To gain insight into when and how context pruning
improves long-form dialogue, we analyze three
factors. First, we compare GPT-4o and GPT-
4.1 to assess how enhanced long-context capabil-
ities in recent LLMs affect the benefits of prun-
ing. We attribute observed shifts in performance
to dialogue-level characteristics, such as dialogue
length and the position of relevant context (Sec-
tion 6.1). Second, to understand what makes prun-
ing effective, we examine different retrieval con-
figurations, focusing on the trade-off between re-
call and precision (Section 6.2). Third, we inves-
tigate whether continuity-preserving turns—those
with low but positive relevance—meaningfully
contribute to answer quality. To this end, we
perform an ablation study that progressively re-
moves the least-relevant turns within the segments
selected by DYCP, analyzing how systematically
dropping these turns affects downstream perfor-
mance (Section 6.3). All analyses in this section
are conducted on the LoCoMo benchmark.

6.2 Recall or Precision?
To understand what makes pruning effective, we
examine how different retrieval configurations in-
fluence the trade-off between recall and precision,
and how this trade-off, in turn, affects both an-
swer quality and latency. Figure 9 shows the re-
lationship between retrieval recall, precision, and
final answer quality across different retrieval con-
figurations in DYCP. We observe a strong posi-
tive correlation between recall and answer qual-
ity: as recall increases, from Top-1 to Dynamic
selection, answer quality steadily improves. This
trend suggests that ensuring the inclusion of rele-
vant content (high recall), even at the cost of intro-
ducing some mildly irrelevant information (lower
precision), is critical for downstream answer gen-
eration. However, further increases in recall yield
diminishing improvements, indicating that retriev-
ing additional segments beyond a certain point of-
fers limited value. Furthermore, because higher
recall typically increases response latency (Fig-
ure 10), the goal is not simply to maximize recall,
but to identify the point at which additional con-
text no longer contributes meaningful gains.
Figure 9: Relationship between retrieval recall,
precision, and final answer quality in DYCP.
Figure 10: Relationship between retrieval recall
and response latency.
These observations also help explain why the con-
cept underlying DYCP’s dynamic span selection
is effective. Unlike fixed top-k approaches, DYCP
expands the retrieved span only when the rele-
vance dynamics justify it—adding segments only
when sustained positive gain is detected. When
relevance drops, the method stops expanding the
span, preventing unnecessary context accumula-
tion. This form of adaptive control maintains high
recall where it matters while avoiding the latency
penalty associated with uniformly larger contexts.
In this sense, the concept of DYCP provides a
principled mechanism for balancing recall and ef-
ficiency in long-form, multi-topic dialogue.
6.3 Ablation on Continuity-Preserving Turns
To evaluate the contribution of low-relevance but
continuity-preserving turns in DYCP, we conduct
an ablation study that progressively removes the
least-relevant turns from each selected segment
while measuring the resulting changes in answer
quality and latency. These removed turns corre-
spond to low but positive relevance scores—turns
that are not directly needed for answering the cur-
rent user query but help maintain local continuity
and discourse flow within the segment. When re-
moval would leave a segment empty, we retain the
single most relevant turn to ensure that each seg-
ment preserves at least one anchor point.
Figure 11 shows that removing these turns leads
to small but consistent drops in answer quality, in-
dicating that even weakly relevant turns contribute
useful contextual signals that support downstream
generation. Latency decreases slightly as more
turns are removed, as expected from the shorter
input. Overall, the trend suggests that these low-
relevance turns help maintain a more coherent lo-
cal context, providing modest but meaningful ben-
efits despite their limited direct relevance.

7 Discussion
We raise the question: "Do LLMs meaningfully
process far less context than their actual input to-
ken limits?" While models like GPT-4o support
up to 128k tokens, our results indicate that they
struggle to fully utilize even 25k tokens, espe-
cially when relevant information appears earlier
in the input. This observation is consistent with
prior findings such as An et al. (2024b), who show
that effective context lengths often fall short, typ-
ically not exceeding half of their training lengths.
Interestingly, GPT-4.1, with its 1M token limit,
handles the same 25k-token dialogues much more
reliably. This contrast suggests that token limits
may reflect throughput capacity rather than effec-
tive working memory, and that in practice, models
may attend to only a smaller, attention-weighted
subset of the input. Several factors may con-
tribute to this discrepancy, including the challenge
of learning long-range dependencies during train-
ing, position-sensitive encoding, and attention sat-
uration. Thus, while token limits continue to grow,
effective long-context understanding may remain
limited, both due to architectural bottlenecks and
practical concerns such as inference cost.
8 Conclusion
In this work, we present DYCP, a dynamic con-
text pruning method for long-form dialogue that
selects query-relevant segments in real time, with-
out pre-segmentation or incurring additional LLM
calls. Across three benchmarks, DYCP consis-
tently improves answer quality while significantly
reducing response latency. Our findings show that,
high recall is more beneficial than precision for re-
trieval, and preserving the sequential nature of di-
alogue plays a key role in response generation. We
also show that even as LLMs improve in handling
longer contexts, context management remains cru-
cial for both response quality and latency. We of-
fer a simple yet effective segment-level context
management strategy that is dynamic, scalable,
and LLM-agnostic, making it well-suited for real-
world deployment. Our research materials, in-
cluding the source code, the LoCoMo split (Sec-
tion 5.1), and all outputs from each experimen-
tal unit (Section 5.5.1), are available at: https:
